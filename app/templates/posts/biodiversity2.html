Mapping Plant Observation Patterns Around Falcon Sightings

An exploratory spatial analysis combining iNaturalist observations, QGIS, and a random-location comparison to investigate how recorded plant taxa are distributed around falcon sightings in the Ithaca, New York region.

Project overview

Using spatial data to ask ecological questions

This project explores whether the plant taxa recorded near falcon observations differ from those recorded near randomly generated locations. It brings together biodiversity records from iNaturalist and spatial analysis in QGIS to build a reproducible workflow for investigating patterns in local biodiversity data.

The project began with a practical question: can publicly contributed species observations be connected spatially to reveal patterns that might otherwise be difficult to see? By creating buffers around falcon observations and comparing them with buffers around random locations, the analysis establishes an initial framework for exploring the distribution of nearby plant records.

Interpretation: This is an exploratory analysis of recorded observations, not a test of plant abundance, falcon diet, habitat preference, or a causal ecological relationship. iNaturalist records show where organisms were documented, not necessarily where they are most abundant.
Research direction

Research question & objectives

Are certain plant taxa disproportionately represented in iNaturalist observations surrounding falcon observation locations compared with randomly selected locations in the Ithaca study area?

The word represented refers here to the frequency of recorded plant observations. It does not imply that a plant is ecologically preferred by falcons or more abundant in the landscape.

01 · Characterize

Identify and summarize plant observation records located within the falcon-associated buffer areas.

02 · Compare

Create a random-location comparison group within the same study boundary and summarize its nearby plant records.

03 · Evaluate

Use descriptive statistics and planned R analyses to examine differences while accounting for uneven sampling and spatial dependence.

04 · Improve

Identify how GIS methods, data quality, and sampling design can be strengthened for future biodiversity research.

Data & methods

GIS workflow

The workflow combines point observations, polygon buffers, spatial selection, and taxonomic summaries. The project was organized in QGIS, with iNaturalist records collected and prepared as georeferenced observation datasets.

1

Collect falcon observations

Compile 124 falcon observation locations within the study boundary. The records include observation identifiers, taxonomic names, dates, quality classifications, and coordinates.

2

Collect plant observations

Retrieve georeferenced plant records from iNaturalist, retaining taxonomic labels, observation identifiers, dates, quality classifications, and coordinates.

3

Create falcon-associated buffers

Create a buffer around each falcon location. The QGIS project used EPSG:4326 (WGS 84), with an entered distance of approximately 0.0009 degrees, intended to approximate 100 meters.

4

Generate random comparison locations

Generate 124 random points within the study-area polygon and create a corresponding set of buffers using the same approximate angular distance.

5

Associate plant observations spatially

Use spatial relationships in QGIS to identify plant records falling within the falcon-associated and random buffer areas.

6

Summarize and compare

Create individual-observation and summary outputs, then compare taxonomic-label frequencies and the distribution of observations across buffers.

Distance and coordinate note: 0.0009 degrees is an approximation, not a precise 100-meter radius. Degrees of longitude and latitude represent different ground distances, and longitude distance varies with latitude. The existing buffers are retained for this preliminary version; a projected-meter workflow (for example, UTM Zone 18N, EPSG:32618) is a recommended validation step.

Next analytical phase

Statistical analysis in R

In development · No inferential results reported yet

The next phase is to use R to move beyond raw observation totals and evaluate the structure of the data at the buffer level. The analysis should preserve unique observation identifiers, distinguish unique observations from repeated buffer associations, and account for the fact that falcon buffers are spatially clustered and overlap.

1 · Data validation

  • Check unique observation IDs and duplicate records.
  • Confirm the unit of analysis: observation, buffer, or buffer-observation association.
  • Reconcile individual plant layers with summary layers.
  • Check dates, taxonomic rank, and observation quality.

2 · Descriptive analysis

  • Calculate plant-record counts per buffer.
  • Summarize zero-observation buffers and count distributions.
  • Compare taxonomic-label frequencies and shared labels.
  • Report medians, ranges, and uncertainty where appropriate.

3 · Sensitivity analysis

  • Repeat summaries using Research Grade records only.
  • Compare species-level records with broader taxonomic labels.
  • Examine whether results change when overlapping falcon buffers are handled differently.
  • Consider date-matched or season-matched comparisons.

4 · Statistical comparison

  • Choose a model appropriate for count data and excess zeros, if present.
  • Assess overdispersion and spatial dependence.
  • Use a defensible comparison design before interpreting p-values.
  • Report effect sizes and confidence intervals, not only significance tests.

Critical evaluation

Limitations & interpretation

!

Opportunistic observation data

iNaturalist records are contributed by users rather than collected through a standardized survey. Observer access, interests, activity, and timing can affect where records occur.

!

Unequal record totals

The individual plant layers contain 426 falcon-associated records and 46 random-associated records. Equal numbers of buffer locations do not guarantee equal sampling effort or comparable observation opportunities.

!

Overlapping falcon buffers

The geometry check identified 184 intersecting pairs among falcon buffers and one among random buffers. A plant record can therefore be associated with multiple falcon buffers, so buffer-level records are not necessarily independent.

!

Approximate buffer distance

The buffer operation used approximately 0.0009 degrees in EPSG:4326. This is not a uniform, exact 100-meter radius and should be treated as a limitation of the preliminary spatial workflow.

!

Taxonomic resolution and quality

Some records are identified only to genus or broader taxonomic groups, and many are marked Needs ID. Taxonomic labels are not all equivalent to confirmed species identifications.

!

Summary-layer interpretation

The falcon summary layer reports 1,051 plant-buffer associations, whereas the individual plant layer contains 426 observation records. These are different measures and should not be combined as if they represented the same count.

These limitations do not make the project unusable. They define what can responsibly be concluded from the current data and provide a concrete roadmap for improving the sampling design and analysis.

Continuing the research

Next steps

  1. Validate spatial methods: compare the existing angular buffers with buffers generated in a projected coordinate system using meters.
  2. Reconcile the data tables: document exactly how individual observations, buffer associations, and summary counts relate to one another.
  3. Improve the comparison design: consider matching random locations by land cover, accessibility, and observation effort, and account for clustering.
  4. Complete the R analysis: conduct descriptive, sensitivity, and appropriately designed statistical analyses.
  5. Add environmental context: incorporate land cover, vegetation structure, or other habitat layers to investigate possible landscape explanations.
  6. Document reproducibility: preserve data sources, API query parameters, QGIS processing steps, scripts, and analysis decisions.

The longer-term goal is to develop a repeatable approach for connecting community-contributed biodiversity records with spatial analysis. This project is an initial step toward using computational tools to support ecological research, conservation, and environmental decision-making.
